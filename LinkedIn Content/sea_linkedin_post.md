In April 2024, a heat dome settled over South and Southeast Asia and did not move for three weeks.

Bangkok recorded a heat index of 52°C. Dhaka exceeded 40°C on fourteen consecutive days. Vietnam's north crossed 40°C. The Philippines issued its highest heat index alert across eleven provinces.

Simultaneously.

Bangladesh's garment factories slowed. Agricultural field work was suspended during peak hours across the region. Outdoor construction halted.

None of this appeared as a quantified risk event in any supply chain risk report, trade finance model, or export credit assessment issued during the same period.

South and Southeast Asia supply roughly 40% of global garment exports, a significant share of electronics components, and the majority of global palm oil and rice trade. The workforce producing these goods is predominantly outdoor, informal, and without employer health protection. The ILO projects the region will lose 2.4% of all working hours annually to heat stress by 2030.

That is not a rounding error. That is a structural drag on export volumes, FX earnings, supply chain reliability, and sovereign fiscal accounts — flowing through six distinct pathways that standard financial models do not capture.

In this piece I map those six pathways, with country-level analysis for Bangladesh, Vietnam, Indonesia, and the Philippines, and NGFS Phase 4 scenario analysis through 2050.

This is Article 3 in the Physical Finance series on heat stress and labour productivity across Asia and global EM.
