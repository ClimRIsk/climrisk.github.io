Everyone uses tires. Nobody thinks about what is inside them.

41% of a tire is rubber compounds. Natural rubber cannot be fully substituted. It has properties no synthetic material replicates at scale, particularly for heavy trucks, aviation, and high performance applications.

And 70% of the world's natural rubber comes from Thailand, Indonesia, and Malaysia.

I ran Michelin through a structured CRFM framework. Here is what you find when you look past the Scope 1 and 2 numbers.

Here is what becomes visible when you run the physical and transition risk:

• The 2011 Thailand floods cut global rubber supply and spiked prices. CMIP6 projections put a repeat event at once every 10 to 15 years by 2035 under moderate warming, compared to once every 25 years historically
• Heat stress above 35 degrees Celsius reduces Hevea rubber tree latex yield. As Southeast Asian temperatures rise, lower altitude plantations contract their productive window
• New rubber plantations take 5 to 7 years to reach maturity. There is no fast adaptation to a supply shock
• EU ETS carbon pricing directly hits European manufacturing and synthetic rubber feedstock costs under a Net Zero 2050 pathway
• But here is the upside nobody is pricing: EVs are 300 to 600 kg heavier than ICE vehicles. EV tires wear 20 to 30% faster. In a world that electrifies fast, Michelin sells more tires per vehicle per year

• How supply chain physical hazard is mapped at the plantation and logistics level using WRI Aqueduct and NASA CMIP6 data
• How rubber yield reduction probability is modelled by decade under RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5

Insight: Climate transition does not hit every business the same way. For some it is pure cost. For Michelin, faster tire wear from EVs is a structural revenue tailwind embedded in the same decarbonisation pathway that raises their manufacturing and feedstock costs. That split only becomes visible when you model both sides in the same DCF.
