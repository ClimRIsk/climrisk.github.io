Luxury goods are built on biological materials. Cashmere, leather, silk, merino wool.

The physical supply chain is not incidental. It is the source of pricing power.

I ran Kering through a structured CRFM framework. Here is what you find when you move past the Environmental P&L and into the actual financial model.

Here is what becomes visible when you run the physical and transition risk:

- Australia's Black Summer fires and drought cut merino wool production by roughly 15% in 2019 to 2020. Premium wool prices spiked. Every luxury house with a wool product felt it in gross margin. Most did not disclose it as a climate risk
- Mongolian cashmere supply faces dzud risk — summer drought followed by severe winter that kills livestock. The 2010 dzud killed 20% of Mongolia's total livestock. IPCC AR6 projects this pattern becoming more frequent under moderate warming
- Premium hide quality is declining across major cattle-raising regions as heat stress reduces the fraction of hides that grade to luxury standard. This is a margin compression mechanism that operates silently
- Northern Italy is Kering's manufacturing heartland. The 2022 Po River drought was the worst in 70 years. Tuscan tanneries depend on river water. That geographic concentration is a physical risk, not a brand asset
- Kering's own Environmental P&L valued natural capital dependency at €1.4 billion in 2022. That number sits outside the income statement and outside every consensus financial model

What it adds is that those materials are increasingly exposed to physical hazard events that compress supply, degrade quality, and spike procurement costs. The EP&L already quantifies this.
