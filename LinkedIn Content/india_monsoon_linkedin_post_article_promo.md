A new El Niño is forming in the Pacific right now.

NOAA's Niño 3.4 data shows sea surface temperatures rising ahead of the pace of recent warming years. The trajectory points toward a significant event by late 2026.

Here is why that matters to anyone managing capital.

India Meteorological Department records going back to 1871 show the same sequence every time a moderate to strong El Niño forms: Walker Circulation weakens, Indian Ocean convection shifts, monsoon rainfall drops below normal. In 2009 that deficit hit 23 percent — the worst in 27 years. In 2023, India imposed rice export restrictions that the FAO Rice Market Monitor recorded as pushing global prices to a 15-year high. India supplies 40 percent of global rice trade. The price signal reached Bangladesh, Senegal, and the Philippines within weeks.

This is not a climate story. It is a financial transmission story.

I mapped the six pathways through which a monsoon deficit moves from atmospheric physics into financial statements:

Agricultural commodity markets, where rice, sugar, and cotton face simultaneous supply disruption in a single season. Agricultural credit, where the Reserve Bank of India's Financial Stability Reports document NPA ratios rising 50 to 100 basis points above trend in deficit years. Sovereign fiscal stress, because 800 million people are covered by India's National Food Security Act and food subsidy costs rise mechanically when output drops. FMCG corporate earnings, where rural India drives 35 to 40 percent of revenues for companies like HUL, ITC, and Dabur. Hydropower and energy, where the Central Electricity Authority records 25 percent of India's electricity from hydro — a deficit forces thermal substitution. And emerging market contagion, because food-importing sovereigns from Sub-Saharan Africa to the Middle East face import cost spikes when India exits the market.

IPCC AR6 Chapter 8 is explicit on what comes next: El Niño events do not become more frequent under warming. They become financially larger as the baseline temperature rises. A moderate El Niño in 2040 operating on a 2.5°C warmer ocean produces agricultural disruptions that exceed anything in the 150-year IMD record.

The physical signal in 2023 was visible in April. The Walker Circulation data was there. The IMD forecast flagged uneven spatial distribution in June. The financial consequences arrived in July. Markets priced them as a surprise.

They were not a surprise. They were physics.
